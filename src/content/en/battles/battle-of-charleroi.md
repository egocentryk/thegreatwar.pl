The Battle of [Charleroi](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charleroi), also known as the Battle of the Sambre, was fought on 21–23 August 1914 in Belgium, in the angle between the rivers [Sambre](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambra) and Meuse. It was one of the [Battles of the Frontiers](/bitwy/bitwa-graniczna), which decided the course of the first phase of the war in the west.

## Lanrezac's warnings

From the start of the war the commander of the French Fifth Army, General Charles Lanrezac, had warned the high command that the Germans were advancing through Belgium in much greater strength than had been thought. Joffre long dismissed these warnings. Only when it became clear that the German right wing was outflanking the French armies from the north was the Fifth Army moved to the Sambre, on the left wing of the French forces, next to the expeditionary force arriving from Britain.

Facing the French were two German armies: the Second Army under General [Karl von Bülow](/postacie/karl-von-bulow), advancing from the north across the Sambre, and the Third Army under General [Max von Hausen](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_von_Hausen), approaching from the east, from the Meuse.

## Course of the battle

On 21 August German troops seized several crossings over the Sambre before the French could occupy them. On 22 August the French infantry tried to throw the Germans back across the river, but its counter-attacks broke down under fire and losses were very heavy. Meanwhile German artillery was already shelling the forts of the besieged fortress of [Namur](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twierdza_Namur), which guarded the confluence of the rivers.

On 23 August Hausen's army forced the Meuse near [Dinant](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinant), threatening Lanrezac's right flank. That same day German soldiers carried out a mass execution of the inhabitants of Dinant, one of the worst crimes against civilians during the invasion of Belgium. The French Fifth Army was in danger of being encircled. On the evening of 23 August Lanrezac gave the order to retreat, without fully coordinating it with the British, who had been fighting at Mons that day.

## Significance

The retreat saved the Fifth Army from destruction, but it exposed the right flank of the British Expeditionary Force and forced the British to retreat as well. On 25 August the fortress of Namur fell. The Great Retreat of the French and British forces began, and it was halted only on the Marne. Although his warnings had proved correct, Lanrezac was removed from his post at the beginning of September. He was replaced by General [Louis Franchet d’Espérey](/postacie/louis-franchet-d-esperey).
