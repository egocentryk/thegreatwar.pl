On 8 August 1914 the British cruiser HMS Astraea shelled [Dar es Salaam](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dar_es_Salaam), the capital of [German East Africa](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niemiecka_Afryka_Wschodnia). Its target was the wireless station through which German warships in the Indian Ocean kept in contact with the shore. This was the beginning of hostilities in East Africa, the longest campaign of the First World War.

## Bombardment and truce

Astraea's shells destroyed the wireless mast. The British also immobilised two German merchant ships lying in the harbour. Fearing a landing, the Germans scuttled a floating dock in the harbour entrance. In doing so they also closed the port to their own cruiser [SMS Königsberg](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Königsberg_(1905)), which was operating in the Indian Ocean.

After the bombardment Astraea's captain agreed to a truce on condition that Dar es Salaam remained an open town and was not used for military purposes. The colony's governor, [Heinrich Schnee](/postacie/heinrich-schnee), readily agreed. He believed that war in Africa would bring the colonies nothing but destruction, and hoped that neutrality could be preserved.

## Lettow-Vorbeck

The commander of the German colonial troops, Lieutenant-Colonel [Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck](/postacie/paul-von-lettow-vorbeck), took a different view. He believed his task was to tie down as many British forces as possible in Africa, so that they could not fight in Europe. Against the governor's wishes he prepared for war, and in the following months he went over to offensive operations against [British East Africa](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brytyjska_Afryka_Wschodnia), today's Kenya.

## The longest campaign

The war in East Africa lasted throughout the First World War. Lettow-Vorbeck's forces, made up mostly of African soldiers known as askaris, avoided destruction for four years by much larger British, Belgian and Portuguese forces. The campaign claimed enormous numbers of victims, especially among the civilian population and African porters. Lettow-Vorbeck laid down his arms only on 25 November 1918, two weeks after the armistice in Europe, when news of the end of the war reached him.
