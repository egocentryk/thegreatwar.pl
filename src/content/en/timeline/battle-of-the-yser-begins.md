On 16 October 1914 the first German units attacked [Diksmuide](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diksmuide), a small town on the river [Yser](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/IJzer) held by the Belgians and French marines. The Belgians date the [Battle of the Yser](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitwa_nad_Yser) from this day, and the British chronology of the war, recording it under this date, notes that it is the Belgian date. The main German attack began two days later. At stake was whether the Belgian army would hold the last strip of its country and whether the Germans would break through along the coast to the Channel ports.

## An army on the river

The Belgian army reached the Yser utterly exhausted. After the [fall of Antwerp](/bitwy/oblezenie-antwerpii) it had retreated through Flanders, [abandoned the ports on the coast](/belgowie-opuszczaja-ostende-i-zeebrugge), and on 14 and 15 October it took up the line of the river from Diksmuide to the sea. It numbered six weak infantry divisions and a cavalry division, by various estimates from about 50,000 to 70,000 men, and it was short of ammunition, guns and officers. King [Albert I](/postacie/albert-i) yielded to French pressure and agreed to fight here. In an order of the day he called on his soldiers to regard as a traitor anyone who spoke of retreat without orders.

The defensive line was not strong. The Yser, flowing between embankments across the flat plain of the polders, was barely a dozen or so metres wide here. The Belgians held several advanced posts on the eastern bank, in the villages of [Mannekensvere](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mannekensvere), Schoore, Keiem and Beerst, and at [Nieuport](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nieuwpoort_(Belgia)) a bridgehead reaching to [Lombardsijde](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lombardsijde). At the southern end, in Diksmuide, stood Rear-Admiral Pierre Ronarc'h's French brigade of fusiliers marins, some 6,500 men, together with Colonel Alphonse Jacques's Belgian brigade. Ronarc'h was ordered to hold the town and its crossing for a few days. He held it for more than three weeks.

## First clashes

Soon after the [Germans reached the coast](/niemcy-docieraja-do-wybrzeza-belgii) and [occupied Ostend](/niemcy-zajmuja-ostende-i-zeebrugge), their columns set off along the roads to the south-west, towards the Yser. This was the route of the new Fourth Army of Duke [Albrecht of Württemberg](/postacie/albrecht-wirtemberski). On the sector from the sea to Diksmuide the III Reserve Corps of General [Hans von Beseler](/postacie/hans-von-beseler), the conqueror of Antwerp, attacked, reinforced by the 4th Ersatz Division. Further south four new reserve corps brought from Germany were approaching.

On 16 October German units came up to Diksmuide and attacked. The sources differ on the details: some speak of an attack on 16 October, others of a reconnaissance that day and an attack the next. They agree that the fusiliers and the Belgians drove the Germans back and that the town remained in Allied hands. In the French and Belgian press the defence of Diksmuide quickly grew into a legend. Meanwhile German patrols prowled in front of the rest of the front, looking for weak points in the Belgian line.

## The attack of 18 October

On 18 October the Fourth Army's main attack began, covering the whole front from the sea to Ypres. German artillery shelled the Belgian advanced posts, and the infantry struck at Keiem, Schoore and Mannekensvere. That same morning Rear-Admiral [Horace Hood's](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace_Hood) British flotilla appeared off Nieuport, and its guns took the German troops on the coast under fire. That day General [Ferdinand Foch](/foch-obejmuje-dowodztwo-we-flandrii) came to Veurne and urged the King to have the Belgians go over to the offensive. Albert refused, pointing to the state of his army.

Over the next few days the Belgians lost all their advanced posts and withdrew behind the river. They blew the bridges, including the bridge in the bend at Tervate, but the Germans stood on the Yser along the whole length of the front. The line of the river, and behind it the Nieuport–Diksmuide railway embankment, were the last obstacle before Dunkirk and Calais. A few days later, when the Germans crossed the river, the Belgians reached for their last weapon: they opened the sluices at Nieuport and let the sea into the plain.
