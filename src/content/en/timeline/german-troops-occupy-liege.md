On 7 August 1914 German troops entered [Liège](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liège). On the third day of the fighting for the Belgian fortress the Germans managed to take the city itself, although the ring of forts surrounding it remained in the defenders' hands.

## Ludendorff's brigade

On the night of 5–6 August German brigades attacked in the gaps between the forts. Most of the attacks broke down under Belgian fire, but the 14th Brigade broke through the defensive line at a point where trenches had not yet been prepared. When its commander was killed, command was taken over by the staff officer [Erich Ludendorff](/postacie/erich-ludendorff). The brigade found itself on the heights above the city, in the very middle of the Belgian defences.

On the morning of 7 August Ludendorff advanced on the antiquated citadel overlooking Liège. The garrison surrendered without a fight. According to accounts, Ludendorff drove up to the gate in a car and demanded its capitulation. Together with the citadel the whole city passed into German hands, along with its bridges over the [Meuse](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moza) and an important railway junction.

## The forts fight on

The capture of the city did not, however, mean the end of the battle. The fortress commander, General Gérard Leman, after a German raid on his headquarters, moved to Fort Loncin west of the city and directed the further defence from there. All twelve forts were still holding out, blocking the railway lines the German army needed. King Albert I had already withdrawn the Belgian field division from Liège to prevent its encirclement.

The Germans had to bring up heavy siege artillery, including 420 mm mortars. The last forts fell only on 16 August.

## Significance

The capture of Liège brought fame to Ludendorff, who received for it Prussia's highest military decoration, the Pour le Mérite. A few weeks later he became chief of staff of the Eighth Army in East Prussia, where together with Paul von Hindenburg he won the victory at Tannenberg. For the Belgians the loss of the city was a blow, but the resistance of the forts showed that the country intended to fight.
