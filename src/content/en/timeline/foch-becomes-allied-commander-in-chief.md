On 14 April 1918 General [Ferdinand Foch](/postacie/ferdinand-foch) received the title of "commander-in-chief of the Allied armies in France" (Général en chef des Armées alliées en France). The French and British governments agreed on it in an exchange of letters, without a new conference. The title gave Foch no new powers, but it gave a name to what had been slowly taking shape for three weeks: after almost four years of war the Allied armies on the Western Front had a common commander. That same day Foch exercised his authority in a way the British found painful. At a meeting at [Abbeville](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbeville_(Francja)) he refused Field Marshal [Douglas Haig](/postacie/douglas-haig) relief for the exhausted British divisions which had been holding out for five days against the German offensive in Flanders.

## From Doullens to Beauvais

The road to this title began at the moment of greatest danger. After the British front on the Somme was broken on 21 March, the French Prime Minister [Georges Clemenceau](/postacie/georges-clemenceau) and the British minister Lord [Alfred Milner](/postacie/alfred-milner) [signed an agreement at Doullens on 26 March](/konferencja-w-doullens) entrusting Foch with coordinating the action of the Allied armies. Foch, however, could only advise and persuade. [On 3 April at Beauvais](/konferencja-w-beauvais) the British, French and American governments gave him "the strategic direction of military operations". The commanders-in-chief, Haig, General [Philippe Pétain](/postacie/philippe-petain) and General [John Pershing](/postacie/john-pershing), retained control of the tactical employment of their armies and the right to appeal to their own governments.

The British had long resisted the idea of a common commander. As late as November 1917 Prime Minister [David Lloyd George](/postacie/david-lloyd-george) assured the House of Commons that he was firmly opposed to it. On 9 April 1918, defending the Doullens and Beauvais agreements in Parliament, he explained that a generalissimo in the full sense of the word was unthinkable. Such a commander would have strategic, tactical and administrative authority, and the last of these, the right to appoint and dismiss generals, could not be entrusted to a foreigner at the head of a national army. He nevertheless called unity of strategic direction a fundamental condition of victory.

## The title

According to the British official history, a suitable title for Foch was agreed by correspondence, and from 14 April he bore the style "Général en chef des Armées alliées en France". In English it was usually rendered as "General-in-Chief", because the term "Commander-in-Chief" was used by Haig himself as commander-in-chief of the British forces in France. The next day Haig learned from London that Foch's new title "in no way alters your mutual relations" as previously laid down. Pétain remained commander-in-chief of the French army, and Pershing of the American.

Foch's authority did not extend to the Belgian army. King [Albert I](/postacie/albert-i) personally commanded his troops and under the Belgian constitution could not place them under the orders of a foreign general. Foch could only make requests of the Belgians. That same day he asked the chief of staff of the Belgian army, General Gillain, to prepare to extend the Belgian front southwards, as far as [Ypres](/miejsca/ypres), when General [Herbert Plumer](/postacie/herbert-plumer)'s British Second Army withdrew from its forward positions east of the town. The Belgians agreed to cooperate with the British. The Italian front also remained outside Foch's authority for the time being.

## The Abbeville meeting

Meanwhile the news from Flanders was bad. [On 9 April](/poczatek-bitwy-nad-lys) the Germans attacked on the river Lys, broke the front of the Portuguese Corps and within a few days took Armentières, Merville and a large part of the plain in front of the railway junction of [Hazebrouck](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazebrouck). In an [order of the day](/rozkaz-plecami-do-muru) dated 11 April Haig wrote that his soldiers were fighting with their backs to the wall. The British divisions, most of them still worn out by the battle on the Somme, were melting away, and the distance separating the Germans from Calais and Dunkirk was shrinking. Foch sent north a cavalry corps and two infantry divisions under General Robillot, and the French Tenth Army of General [Paul Maistre](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Maistre) was marching to the Doullens area. For Haig this was not enough.

On Sunday 14 April, at 11 a.m., Haig, his chief of staff General Herbert Lawrence, Foch, his chief of staff General [Maxime Weygand](/postacie/maxime-weygand), the head of the British liaison mission to Foch, General John Du Cane, and Lord Milner, who had come over from England specially, met at Abbeville. Haig presented a note from his staff and explained that because of heavy losses the British divisions were vanishing before his eyes, and the remaining soldiers were utterly tired. The German reserves were intact, the British exhausted. The French reserves, meanwhile, stood too far away to stop the Germans if they made for Calais. Haig was convinced that the German high command intended to decide the war by destroying the British army.

Foch replied by recalling the tenacity with which the British had defended themselves [at Ypres in October and November 1914](/bitwy/pierwsza-bitwa-pod-ypres). He categorically refused to relieve troops in the middle of a battle. Such an operation would immobilise both the relieving and the relieved units for several days, at a time when reserves were barely sufficient. The French reserves, positioned so that they could move wherever the Germans struck, would be badly used if they got stuck in Flanders, and the enemy could attack at any point on the front. Finally, to the astonishment of those present, he declared: "La bataille d'Hazebrouck est finie" (the Battle of Hazebrouck is over).

Before the end of the meeting Haig wrote out his demands by hand and handed them to Foch. He asked for four divisions of the Tenth Army to be moved to the [Béthune](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Béthune)–[Lillers](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lillers) line, with General Micheler's Fifth Army troops behind them, so that they could support the British along the whole sector from Hazebrouck to the Somme. In the evening Foch replied that one division of the Tenth Army would be placed north of [Frévent](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frévent), from where it could reach a threatened sector within a day if necessary, and that a suitable place was being sought for a second. Haig answered in writing that he considered these steps insufficient and demanded the further transfer of French troops to the Cassel area, because the Germans were throwing fresh divisions from their reserve into the battle.

The next morning Foch explained his reasoning to Clemenceau and Milner. According to his intelligence the Germans had 48 divisions in reserve, more than before the start of the offensive in Flanders, and could strike at short notice anywhere between Noyon and Arras. He considered the sector between [Montdidier](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montdidier_(Somma)) and the river Luce particularly threatened, since German divisions stood thickly there and had not begun to dig in. The British official history admits that Foch's decision cost the British army many lives and its commanders many anxious hours, but judges that his appreciation of the situation and his resistance to very strong pressure command admiration. The British front was bent but not broken, and since the Portuguese had left the line on 9 April the Germans had been advancing slowly.

## Significance

Foch was not a generalissimo in the full sense of the word. He could not appoint or dismiss generals of foreign armies, and every commander-in-chief could appeal against his orders to his own government. His authority rested on trust and on the fact that he controlled the French reserves. The dispute at Abbeville showed, however, that he did not intend to be merely a supplier of reinforcements to whoever asked for them most loudly. He wanted to husband his reserves for a future counter-offensive and to throw them in where the fate of the whole front was being decided. In the very next days, as the Germans approached the hills of Flanders, French divisions began to take over British sectors near Kemmel.

Foch's powers gradually grew. On 2 May, at a session of the Supreme War Council at Abbeville, his coordinating authority was extended to the Italian front as well, although the Italian Prime Minister [Vittorio Emanuele Orlando](/postacie/vittorio-emanuele-orlando) would not agree to hand him command of the Italian army. In the summer, when the Allies went over to the offensive, Foch received the baton of a Marshal of France, and in the autumn he led the Allied armies to victory.
