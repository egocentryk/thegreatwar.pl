On 24 August 1914 Austria-Hungary broke off diplomatic relations with [Japan](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesarstwo_Wielkiej_Japonii). The day before, Japan had [declared war on Germany](/japonia-wypowiada-wojne-niemcom), and Vienna decided to stand by its ally in the Far East as well.

## A cruiser at Tsingtao

Austria-Hungary's only sizeable warship in Asian waters was the old cruiser [SMS Kaiserin Elisabeth](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_Kaiserin_Elisabeth). At the outbreak of war she lay in the German port of Tsingtao, present-day [Qingdao](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qingdao). In mid-August, while Vienna still hoped to avoid a conflict with Japan, the ship was disarmed and most of her crew were sent to [Tientsin](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiencin).

Soon, however, a new order arrived. Emperor [Franz Joseph I](/postacie/franciszek-jozef-i) instructed the cruiser's captain to take part in the fighting together with the German navy. The sailors returned to Tsingtao, and the monarchy broke off relations with Japan. The Austro-Hungarian ambassador in Washington passed this news to the United States government on 26 August. The ambassador in Tokyo, Baron Ladislaus Müller von Szentgyörgy, left Japan.

## Subsequent events

Japan responded at once. On 25 August it announced that it was in a state of war with Austria-Hungary. Part of the crew of the Kaiserin Elisabeth reinforced the fortress garrison, and some of the guns were removed from the ship and set up on land. During the [siege of Tsingtao](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblężenie_Tsingtau) the cruiser supported the defenders with her fire. On 2 November 1914, having run out of ammunition, her crew scuttled her in the harbour, and a few days later Tsingtao capitulated. The Austro-Hungarian sailors went into Japanese captivity together with the Germans and returned home only in 1920.
