On 21 February 1916 Lieutenant-General Sir Nevil Macready ceased to be Adjutant-General of the [British Expeditionary Force](/sily-zbrojne/brytyjski-korpus-ekspedycyjny). Of the four generals who in August 1914 had headed the [force's headquarters](/sztab-brytyjskiego-korpusu-ekspedycyjnego), he alone still held his original post at that time. He was leaving, however, not in disgrace but on promotion: on the same day in London he took over from General [Henry Sclater](/dymisja-henryego-sclatera) the office of Adjutant-General of the whole army.

## A staff officer for people

Macready, born in 1862, the son of the famous actor William Charles Macready, had served from his youth in the Scottish regiment of the Gordon Highlanders. During the Boer War he went through the [siege of Ladysmith](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblężenie_Ladysmith), and before the war he had directed personnel matters at the War Office. He also dealt with the use of troops in aid of the civil power, among other occasions during the miners' strikes in Wales in 1910. In the spring of 1914, during the crisis over Irish Home Rule, he took command in Belfast.

In August 1914 he landed with his staff at [Le Havre](/miejsca/hawr) and for a year and a half headed the Adjutant-General's branch at headquarters. As he himself described it, in the field this branch was responsible for forming new units, replacing losses, discipline together with the military police and field prisons, military law, the medical services and the evacuation of the wounded, the army chaplains, the registration of graves, and even printing presses. Behind these dry headings lay real problems: overcrowded hospitals, reinforcements for a growing army and so-called trench foot in the winter of 1914–1915. Macready also supported Fabian Ware, whose volunteer team had begun marking and recording the graves of British soldiers. From this initiative there later grew the [Commonwealth War Graves Commission](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komisja_Grobów_Wojennych_Wspólnoty_Narodów).

## Summoned to London

Macready was known for enforcing the regulations without regard to persons. As late as the end of January 1916 his military police detained in the forward zone the Attorney General, Sir F.E. Smith, a member of the government, who had gone without a pass to visit Winston Churchill in the trenches. The affair caused a stir, but did not harm Macready's career.

According to his memoirs, on 10 February the Secretary of State for War, Lord [Kitchener](/postacie/horatio-kitchener), and the Chief of the Imperial General Staff, William Robertson, came to headquarters at [Saint-Omer](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint-Omer_(Pas-de-Calais)), and on 12 February the commander-in-chief, General [Douglas Haig](/postacie/douglas-haig), told him that Kitchener wanted him as Adjutant-General at the War Office. Macready regretted parting with the staff he had worked with since the landing at Le Havre, and thought without enthusiasm of the gloomy atmosphere of Whitehall. On 20 February he crossed to London. His place in France was taken by the force's former Engineer-in-Chief, General George Fowke.
