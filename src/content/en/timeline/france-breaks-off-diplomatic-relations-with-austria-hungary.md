On 10 August 1914 [France](/kraje/francja) broke off diplomatic relations with [Austria-Hungary](/kraje/austro-wegry). On that day the missions of the French ambassador in [Vienna](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiedeń), Alfred Dumaine, and of the Austro-Hungarian ambassador in [Paris](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paryż), Count Nikolaus Szécsen, came to an end. Both diplomats left their posts.

## War without a direct dispute

France had been at war with Germany for a week, yet it still maintained normal relations with Germany's closest ally. France and Austria-Hungary had no common border and no direct dispute. The French army was fighting the Germans in Alsace and Lorraine, while Austria-Hungary was at war with Serbia and preparing for a clash with Russia.

Such a state of affairs could not last long, however. France was the ally of Russia, on which Austria-Hungary had declared war on 6 August. Paris also accused Vienna of sending Austro-Hungarian troops to support the Germans on the Western Front, which the Austrians denied. Maintaining relations with a state at war with one's own ally was politically impossible.

## Declaration of war

The breaking off of relations was the step preceding war. France declared war on Austria-Hungary on 11 or 12 August, depending on the source, and on 12 August Britain did so as well. From that moment all the great powers of the Entente were at war with both Germany and Austria-Hungary.

## Significance

For a long time the Franco-Austrian conflict remained largely formal. The Austro-Hungarian army fought above all against Russia, Serbia and, from 1915, Italy. In the Mediterranean and the Adriatic, however, the French fleet blockaded the Austro-Hungarian fleet, and in the following years French troops fought the Austrians on the Balkan and Italian fronts.
