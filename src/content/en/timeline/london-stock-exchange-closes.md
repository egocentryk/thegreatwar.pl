On Friday 31 July 1914, less than an hour before trading was due to begin, the [London Stock Exchange](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Stock_Exchange) closed its doors. It was the first such closure since the exchange was founded in 1801. London, then the financial centre of the world, stopped trading in securities before Great Britain had even entered the war.

The cause was the panic that had been gripping the financial markets for several days. With each new ultimatum and mobilisation, investors across Europe were selling shares and bonds en masse, trying to convert them into cash and gold. One continental [stock exchange](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giełda_papierów_wartościowych) after another suspended business, and foreign clients began dumping their securities on the London market. The [City of London](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_of_London) faced a wave of bankruptcies among brokers and banks whose continental debtors could not or would not settle their obligations. London was the last of the great European markets to close.

The same day the [Bank of England](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_Anglii) raised its interest rate from 4 to 8 per cent, and the next day to 10 per cent, to stem the outflow of gold. Also on 31 July the [New York Stock Exchange](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Stock_Exchange) closed; it did not resume even limited trading until 12 December 1914. At the beginning of August the British government extended the bank holiday and introduced a moratorium on the repayment of certain obligations to calm the situation.

The London Stock Exchange did not reopen until 4 January 1915, and then under strict restrictions, including a requirement that transactions be settled in cash only. The closure of 31 July showed that the coming war would strike not only armies and states but also the world economy, which before 1914 had been more closely interconnected than ever before.
