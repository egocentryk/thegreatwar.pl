On 5 August 1914, the day after Britain entered the war, Prime Minister [H. H. Asquith](/postacie/herbert-henry-asquith) called a war council at [10 Downing Street](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/10_Downing_Street). For the first time the most important ministers and military commanders sat at one table to decide how Britain would wage the war on land.

## Who sat at the table

Those taking part included the Foreign Secretary, Sir [Edward Grey](/postacie/edward-grey), the First Lord of the Admiralty, [Winston Churchill](/postacie/winston-churchill), and Lord [Haldane](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Haldane). The military side was represented by Field Marshal Lord [Roberts](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederick_Roberts), the commander-in-chief of the Expeditionary Force, Field Marshal Sir [John French](/postacie/john-french), General Sir [Douglas Haig](/postacie/douglas-haig) and other senior officers. Also present was Field Marshal [Lord Kitchener](/postacie/horatio-kitchener), who that same day became Secretary of State for War.

## Where to send the army

The key question was where and when to send the [British Expeditionary Force](/sily-zbrojne/brytyjski-korpus-ekspedycyjny). Pre-war arrangements with France provided for the British to concentrate on the left wing of the French army, around [Maubeuge](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maubeuge) near the Belgian border. French, however, considered sending the troops to [Antwerp](/miejsca/antwerpia), to operate together with the Belgians on the Germans' flank. Haig warned against hastily sending the whole of the small professional army and argued for expanding it.

Kitchener proved the most far-sighted. Unlike most of those at the council, he foresaw that the war would last for years and that Britain would need an army of millions. He also feared that a force concentrated at Maubeuge would find itself in the path of the main German thrust.

## Decisions

The council ended with a decision to send the Expeditionary Force to France. The next day the government settled that four infantry divisions and a cavalry division would cross to the Continent to begin with, while two divisions would stay at home for fear of a German invasion. In the end, after talks with the French, concentration near Maubeuge was agreed.

## From council to War Council

Contemporary chronicles called the meeting of 5 August the first session of the British War Council. The formal War Council, which took over the tasks of the pre-war Committee of Imperial Defence and was given executive powers, was not created until November 1914, as a cabinet committee. It was this body that took the most important strategic decisions in the following months, including the one on the Dardanelles operation. In June 1915 it was replaced by the Dardanelles Committee.
