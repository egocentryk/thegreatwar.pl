On 12 October 1917 at 5.25 a.m., in rain and mud, two divisions of II Anzac Corps advanced on the ridge above the village of [Passchendaele](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passendale_(Zonnebeke)), the last high ground before the plain east of [Ypres](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ieper). The 3rd Australian Division of General [John Monash](/postacie/john-monash) was to take the village itself, and the New Zealand Division the Bellevue spur north of it. The attack, prepared within three days of the [failed attack at Poelcappelle](/poczatek-bitwy-pod-poelcappelle), broke down in front of uncut wire and concrete pillboxes. A handful of Australians reached the church in Passchendaele and turned back without meeting anyone, and in the afternoon both divisions fell back almost to their start line. For [New Zealand](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowa_Zelandia) it was the day with the greatest number of casualties in its entire history.

## The night before the attack

Field Marshal [Douglas Haig](/postacie/douglas-haig) still believed that the Germans in Flanders were close to collapse and wanted to take the ridge before the autumn rains brought the [Ypres offensive](/bitwy/trzecia-bitwa-pod-ypres) to a complete halt. On 11 October he told correspondents that on 9 October the army had been beaten only by the mud. At the front things looked different. The New Zealanders, who had taken over the sector in front of Bellevue the day before, found the slopes strewn with wounded from 9 October, who had been lying there for several days without help, and their patrol saw belts of barbed wire 20–45 metres wide in front of the German pillboxes. They had almost two and a half kilometres to go to the final objective, and the artillery, which had been unable to get forward through the mud, was firing from makeshift positions far to the rear. On the evening of 11 October, after a whole afternoon of rain, the commander of the neighbouring Fifth Army, General Hubert Gough, asked General [Herbert Plumer](/postacie/herbert-plumer) by telephone to postpone the attack. Plumer consulted his corps commanders and refused.

The Australian brigades set out from the fields near Potijze at about 6 p.m. The duckboard tracks along which they marched were shelled with high-explosive and gas shells, and units relieved from the front were coming the other way. That night the Germans carried out a long-planned gas bombardment, but a strong wind dispersed the gas. At about 1.30 a.m. it began to rain. By 3.30 it was pouring. The men moved in single file in total darkness, holding on to the equipment of the man in front. Only on the way did they learn that the line from which they were to advance was not in British hands at all. The New Zealanders crossed the Ravebeek stream on footbridges of coconut matting laid by the engineers and took up their positions on time. The troops on the jumping-off tapes lay under fire, covered with groundsheets, and some of the men fell asleep in the rain.

## Morning: Bellevue

At 5.25 a.m. the barrage opened. It was weak and ragged, some of the shells fell on their own lines, and the howitzer shells buried themselves in the mud and exploded without harming the pillboxes. Machine guns at once opened fire from the German positions on the Bellevue spur and from Crest Farm in front of Passchendaele. At about 6 a.m. a strong wind sprang up and the drizzle turned into a downpour.

The New Zealand 2nd Brigade, with battalions from [Otago](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otago) and [Canterbury](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canterbury_(region)), reached the wire and could not cut it. The only gap led along the sunken road to Gravenstafel, but machine guns placed on either side of it killed everyone who stepped onto it. Battalion after battalion threw itself at the same wire. Some men crawled under two belts of entanglements and were killed just in front of the loopholes of the pillboxes. On the left wing the 3rd Rifle Brigade took the cemetery on the edge of the spur and got as far as Wolf Copse, but at about 8 a.m. its commanders ordered the men to dig in. The only clear success was achieved in the marshes by the Ravebeek by Second Lieutenant Arthur Cockerell, whose platoon took two pillboxes and several dozen prisoners. Only one soldier of the platoon remained with him.

## Morning: by the church in Passchendaele

The Australian 9th Brigade advanced along the ridge beside the road to the village. Disorganised from the first minutes, it lost an hour capturing machine-gun nests, until Captain Clarence Jeffries of the 34th Battalion led men round to the rear of the German positions and took 25 prisoners. When he repeated this at another machine gun, he was killed. He was posthumously awarded the [Victoria Cross](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krzyż_Wiktorii). At about 8.30 a.m. some hundred men from three battalions stood on the eastern slope of the ridge, roughly where the second objective was to run, and sent several hundred prisoners to the rear. In the Ravebeek valley the 10th Brigade was held up near the first objective by fire from the Bellevue spur, which the New Zealanders had not taken.

One group, however, about twenty men, mostly of the 38th Battalion, went on up the valley, because they could see no barrage in front of them to keep close behind. At Crest Farm, on the edge of the village, the garrison of a large pillbox surrendered to them. The Australians reached the church in Passchendaele and found neither Germans nor any of their own men there. They turned back. On the way back they saw the Germans returning to Crest Farm and opening fire from it. One of the division's units carried an Australian flag that it was to plant in the village. It was not planted.

## Afternoon: the retreat

News reached the headquarters with delays of many hours. Monash believed until noon that his division was advancing, and he learned that the New Zealanders were held up along their whole front only shortly before eleven. Corps ordered the New Zealanders to renew the attack at 3 p.m. The battalion commanders, convinced that it was a death sentence for what was left of their brigades, were already preparing to go with their men when the order was cancelled.

At about 3 p.m. it began to rain again. The Australians on the ridge, fired on directly by German guns and from the flank from the Bellevue spur, received no reply to the messages they had been sending since morning. Major Giblin in the Ravebeek valley, seeing Germans infiltrating behind his left flank, withdrew his men in small groups, and by 3.30 p.m. they were back on the start line. On the ridge the retreat began at about 3.45 p.m. and was disorderly. The 12th Brigade of the 4th Australian Division, which had taken the railway cutting on the Keiberg hill and repelled two German counter-attacks, had to withdraw along the embankment when it lost its cover on the left. By the evening the front ran practically where it had in the morning. In the north the units of the Fifth Army also for the most part stopped not far from their start lines. Only near the boundary with the French did a brigade of the 17th Division and the Guards advance towards Houthulst Forest.

## The wounded in the mud

The night of 12/13 October was dark and rainy. In the Ravebeek valley the wounded of the 3rd Australian Division lay beside the wounded of 9 October, and an Australian lieutenant who returned there with his battalion the following night counted more than forty dead within twenty yards of himself. A single stretcher needed six or even eight men. At the New Zealand dressing stations dozens of stretchers stood in the rain and hail, and in two dugouts measuring roughly three by four metres, which housed the headquarters of two battalions, 56 men were crowded, most of them wounded. The next day an informal truce settled over the slopes. German riflemen let the stretcher-bearers work, and the last of the wounded were carried out only on 14 October.

## The day's toll

On 12 October the New Zealand Division lost about 2,700 men, including about 845 killed or mortally wounded. In a few hours more New Zealanders died than on any other day in the country's history. The division, which in June had taken the village of [Messines](/bitwy/bitwa-pod-messines-1917), suffered its only great defeat. The 3rd Australian Division lost 3,199 men according to the Australian official historian Charles Bean, and the 12th Brigade about a thousand. Total British and Dominion losses that day are estimated at about 13,000. The German 195th Division, which defended Passchendaele, lost more than 3,300 men on 9 and 12 October. The Crown Prince of Bavaria, [Rupprecht](/postacie/rupprecht-bawarski), commanding the German armies in Flanders, noted that the rain was his "most effective ally".

On 13 October the British command decided to suspend attacks until the weather improved and the roads were repaired. A few days later the exhausted II Anzac Corps was replaced by the Canadian Corps of General [Arthur Currie](/postacie/arthur-currie), whom Haig entrusted with the capture of Passchendaele.
