On the morning of 4 August 1914 news reached London that German troops had crossed the Belgian frontier at Gemmenich. For the British government this was a line that could not be crossed without a response. The Foreign Secretary, [Sir Edward Grey](/postacie/edward-grey), instructed the ambassador in Berlin, Sir Edward Goschen, to demand from [Germany](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesarstwo_Niemieckie) an assurance that it would respect the neutrality of [Belgium](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgia). The answer had to arrive by midnight Berlin time, that is by 11 p.m. in London.

[Great Britain](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zjednoczone_Królestwo_Wielkiej_Brytanii_i_Irlandii) recalled that Germany, like Britain itself, had signed the [Treaty of London of 1839](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traktat_londyński_(1839)), guaranteeing Belgium's independence and neutrality. It also pointed out that France had given such an assurance a few days earlier, while Germany had refused it. If Berlin did not give a satisfactory reply, Great Britain would have to take all steps in defence of the treaty.

In the evening Goschen went to see Chancellor [Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg](/postacie/theobald-von-bethmann-hollweg) to discuss the situation one last time. The chancellor was agitated. According to the ambassador's account, he declared that Great Britain was going to make war on a kindred nation for the sake of one word, "neutrality", and for "a scrap of paper". The phrase quickly went round the world. British propaganda presented it as proof that Germany did not respect the agreements it had concluded.

Earlier the same day, in fact, Bethmann Hollweg had admitted in the Reichstag that the entry into Belgium violated international law, and had justified it with the words "necessity knows no law". Germany therefore had no intention of withdrawing its troops. No satisfactory reply arrived by midnight, and the ultimatum turned into war.
