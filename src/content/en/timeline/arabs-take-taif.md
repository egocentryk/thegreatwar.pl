On 22 September 1916 the Turkish garrison of [Ta'if](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/At-Ta’if), a mountain town south-east of Mecca, surrendered unconditionally to the forces of Emir [Abdullah](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_Allah_I_ibn_Husajn), the second son of the Sharif of Mecca, [Hussein bin Ali](/postacie/husajn-ibn-ali). The Turkish Governor-General of the [Hejaz](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidżaz), Ghalib Pasha, was taken prisoner. This is the date given for the capitulation by the British official history of the campaign in Egypt and Palestine (MacMunn and Falls) and by the Turkish historian Mustafa Bostancı. The Lebanese historian George Antonius and Ahmad al-Siba'i, the author of a history of Mecca, give 21 September. The Sharif announced the victory in a communiqué that Reuters circulated from Cairo only a few days later.

## The governor's summer capital

Ta'if lies in the mountains, about 110 kilometres from Mecca. Because of its cooler climate the Turkish Governor-General moved there every summer with most of the Mecca garrison. So it was in 1916 too, which made it much easier for the insurgents to [take Mecca](/wybuch-powstania-w-mekce). Ta'if, on the other hand, was a much harder target. According to the British official history the town was strongly fortified and surrounded by trenches, and the garrison numbered 3,000 soldiers with ten 75-millimetre Krupp guns. Bostancı writes of 3,500 soldiers.

Abdullah began the siege of the town in June, at the same time as the rising broke out in Mecca. According to MacMunn and Falls he had about 5,000 tribal fighters, but he did not attempt an assault, because the poorly armed and undisciplined Bedouin stood no chance against trenches and artillery. He confined himself to sealing off the town. Early reports from Cairo, repeated in June by the press all over the world, that Ta'if had fallen and only two small forts were holding out, proved very premature.

## The Egyptian guns

What changed the situation was artillery. As [at Mecca](/bitwy/bitwa-o-mekke), it came from across the Red Sea: Egyptian mountain batteries and a battery of previously captured Turkish howitzers, sent from Egypt and likewise manned by Egyptians. On 16 July, five weeks after the siege began, the guns opened fire and quickly silenced two Turkish guns in advanced positions north of the town. The next day they were moved closer to the fortifications, which they shelled for ten days. The Turkish guns were completely dominated and the fortifications seriously damaged.

The Arabs threatened an assault several times, but none took place. The British official history considers that Abdullah had no intention of assaulting at all. He knew that the garrison was bound to fall anyway, and that heavy losses might dishearten his fighters. Antonius likewise writes that the emir wisely waited. The siege therefore dragged on for another two months, with intermittent shelling. The Turks proposed a ceasefire several times, but Abdullah rejected every offer, until finally the garrison surrendered unconditionally.

## The capitulation

According to the Sharif's communiqué, published by Reuters, 50 officers and 1,800 men were taken prisoner at Ta'if, and the victors captured 10 guns and large military stores. The most valuable prisoner was Ghalib Pasha. According to MacMunn and Falls the Sharif by then held more than 5,000 prisoners, according to Antonius about 6,000. Some of them, Arabs and Syrians, volunteered to fight against the Turks.

The Ottoman authorities long concealed the defeat. Antonius quotes a communiqué that the Damascus daily *Al-Sharq* published on 24 September, three days after the fall of the town. It claimed that all was quiet at Ta'if and that the Bedouin under Emir Abdullah had been repulsed with heavy losses.

## Significance

The capture of Ta'if closed the first stage of the [Arab Revolt](/bitwy/powstanie-arabskie). The Sharif held all the main towns of the Hejaz except [Medina](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medyna_(miasto)), where the strong garrison of Fahreddin Pasha, linked by rail to Damascus, not only held out but also drove the Arabs back from the town's surroundings. Antonius notes that after the fall of Ta'if and the successful pilgrimage to Mecca at the beginning of October, attended by many Muslims from Egypt, Cairo began to treat the revolt as a serious blow to Turkey. The following months, however, proved the hardest for the insurgents, because the Turks from Medina threatened the road to Mecca.
