On 2 August 1914 at 7 p.m. the German minister in [Brussels](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruksela), Claus von Below-Saleske, handed the Belgian Foreign Minister, Julien Davignon, a note that sealed the fate of [Belgium](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgia). [Germany](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesarstwo_Niemieckie) demanded permission for its troops to march through Belgian territory. It gave twelve hours for a reply.

The justification was a pretext. The German government claimed to have reliable information that the French army intended to enter Belgium in order to attack Germany, and presented its demand as a pre-emptive measure. In reality the march through Belgium had for years been the basis of the German [war plan](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plan_Schlieffena), which envisaged bypassing the heavily fortified Franco-German border from the north.

The note combined promises with a threat. If Belgium maintained a "benevolent neutrality" and did not oppose the passage of troops, Germany undertook after the war to respect its independence and territory and to pay compensation for all damage. If, however, it resisted, it would be treated as an enemy, and its future would be decided by force of arms.

The demand was an open violation of the neutrality of Belgium, of which Prussia was one of the guarantors under the [Treaty of London of 1839](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traktat_londyński_(1839)). That same night King [Albert I](/postacie/albert-i) summoned a meeting of the government and the leading politicians. It was unanimously decided to reject the ultimatum, even at the cost of war with the most powerful army in Europe.

The reply, delivered on the morning of 3 August, was a firm refusal. Belgium declared that accepting the German demands would mean sacrificing the nation's honour and betraying its duties towards Europe. On 4 August German troops crossed the Belgian border. The ultimatum of 2 August became one of the key reasons why Great Britain entered the war.
