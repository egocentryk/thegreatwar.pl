On the night of 21–22 July 1917 German gendarmes arrested in Warsaw [Józef Piłsudski](/postacie/jozef-pilsudski), the creator of the [Legions](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legiony_Polskie_(1914–1918)) and until recently head of the military department of the [Provisional Council of State](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tymczasowa_Rada_Stanu). Detained with him was his closest associate and chief of staff, Colonel [Kazimierz Sosnkowski](/postacie/kazimierz-sosnkowski). Some accounts place the arrest on the morning of 22 July. Both were immediately taken out of the city. The man who in August 1914 had led the first Polish unit out of Kraków to fight Russia alongside the Central Powers had become a prisoner of Germany.

## Why Piłsudski

The arrest was a direct consequence of the [oath crisis](/kryzys-przysiegowy). Two weeks earlier, on 9 and 11 July, legionaries of the I and III Brigades, at Piłsudski's call, had refused to swear an oath of "brotherhood in arms" with Germany and Austria-Hungary, and the German occupation authorities had disarmed and interned them. The plan of Governor-General [Hans Hartwig von Beseler](/postacie/hans-von-beseler), who wanted to build a large Polish army at Germany's side, lay in ruins. The Germans had no doubt who was behind it.

They were particularly worried by the [Polish Military Organisation](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polska_Organizacja_Wojskowa) (POW), a clandestine organisation answering to Piłsudski, which in January had formally placed itself under the Council of State and was now changing sides. Justifying the arrest to the Council of State, Beseler pointed precisely to the change in the POW's attitude under Piłsudski's direction and argued that the situation called for swift action: behind the Eastern Front the Germans would not tolerate an organisation that might turn against them. In the summer of 1917 the occupation authorities also arrested other activists of the independence camp, among them [Walery Sławek](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walery_Sławek), [Wacław Jędrzejewicz](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wacław_Jędrzejewicz) and [Adam Skwarczyński](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Skwarczyński).

## The road to Magdeburg

The arrested men were brought under guard to the [Vienna Station](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dworzec_Wiedeński_w_Warszawie) and taken in a second-class compartment via [Posen](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poznań) to [Danzig](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gdańsk). According to accounts collected by the German-Polish portal Porta Polonica, Piłsudski travelled in his Legion uniform and Sosnkowski in civilian clothes. They reached Danzig during the night of 22–23 July and were put in the prison on what was then Schiesstange (today Kurkowa Street), where they were held on separate floors. A prison warder recalled that Piłsudski was polite but silent. A week later both were moved on, separately, deeper into Germany: to the prison in [Spandau](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spandau) in Berlin, and from the beginning of August to the fortress at [Wesel](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wesel) on the Rhine.

Piłsudski's last place of imprisonment was [Magdeburg](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdeburg), where he arrived at the end of August. In the [citadel](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twierdza_Magdeburg) he lived in a small wooden house with a garden, where officers of the garrison had previously served their terms of arrest. Sosnkowski was held in another prison in Magdeburg, and for a year the two had no contact. Studies differ on the details of this journey. Some short biographical notes say that Piłsudski was taken from Danzig straight to Magdeburg, but Andrzej Garlicki's biography and archival research mention Spandau and Wesel on the way. Both were taken to Magdeburg around 22–23 August.

## Reactions

The arrest of the Commandant was a blow to the Provisional Council of State, which was losing its raison d'être anyway. Without Piłsudski and without the army it was supposed to create, it lasted barely a month: at the end of August its members resigned their seats. The Polish Military Organisation, whose activists the Germans began to hunt down, went deeper underground and turned ever more clearly against the occupiers.

## Birth of a legend

The Germans wanted to get rid of a troublesome leader, but in reality they gave him what he could not have won as a member of a Council appointed by the occupiers. Until July 1917 Piłsudski's opponents, especially the pro-Entente National Democrats, had portrayed him as a tool of the Central Powers. From then on the prisoner of Magdeburg was a victim of the Germans and a symbol of the struggle for independence against all three partitioning powers. His popularity grew with every month of captivity, even among those who had previously distrusted him.

Piłsudski spent more than a year in Magdeburg. When the German Empire was falling apart in November 1918, the Germans released him together with Sosnkowski and sent them by special train to Warsaw. He arrived there on 10 November, on the eve of Poland's regaining of independence.
