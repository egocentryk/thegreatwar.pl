On 31 July 1914 [Austria-Hungary](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Węgry) ordered the general [mobilisation](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobilizacja) of its army. Its first day was set for 4 August. It was a response to general mobilisation in [Russia](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperium_Rosyjskie), which had dashed Vienna's hopes of a short, local war with Serbia.

Until then the monarchy had carried out only a partial mobilisation, as provided for in the plan for war with [Serbia](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Królestwo_Serbii) and Montenegro. Now it had to prepare for a clash with the largest army in Europe. Russia threatened above all [Galicia](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galicja_(Europa_Środkowa)), where the main front against the Tsar's forces was to be formed. Berlin pressed for the [Austro-Hungarian Army](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armia_Austro-Węgier) to direct its main forces precisely against Russia, because Germany intended to strike at France first and needed a strong ally in the east.

The chief of the general staff, [Franz Conrad von Hötzendorf](/postacie/franz-conrad-von-hotzendorf), found himself in a difficult position. Some of the units that, according to the plans, were to fight in Galicia in the event of war with Russia were already on their way to the Balkans. The railway mobilisation timetables were so rigid that these troops could not be turned back at once. They therefore went first to the Serbian front and only later were transported east. In the decisive first weeks of the war they were missing both in Galicia and in Serbia.

General mobilisation sealed the transition from a Balkan war to a European war. On 6 August Austria-Hungary declared war on Russia, and at the end of August the first great battles were fought in Galicia, in which the Habsburg army suffered heavy losses.
