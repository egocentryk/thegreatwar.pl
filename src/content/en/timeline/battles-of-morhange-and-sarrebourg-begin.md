On 14 August 1914 the main offensive of the French army in [Lorraine](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotaryngia) began. Two armies, the First Army under General [Auguste Dubail](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Dubail) and the Second Army under General [Noël de Castelnau](/postacie/noel-de-castelnau), crossed the border and advanced into the province France had lost in 1871. The fighting that began then later came to be called the Battles of [Morhange](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morhange) and [Sarrebourg](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarrebourg).

## The great offensive

The French war plan envisaged the main blow against Germany being delivered through Lorraine. The Commander-in-Chief, [Joseph Joffre](/postacie/joseph-joffre), and his staff believed that a resolute attack, pressed home with full commitment, would break the enemy's defences. Recovering Lorraine, rich in coal and iron ore, also had enormous symbolic importance for the whole nation.

For the first few days the French advanced almost unhindered. They occupied one place after another, and optimistic reports flowed back to Paris. They did not know that the German Sixth Army of Crown Prince Rupprecht of Bavaria and the Seventh Army were deliberately falling back, drawing them deep into well-prepared ground.

## The trap

On 20 August the Germans launched a massive counter-attack. The French infantry, advancing in close formation and in red trousers, came under fire from heavy artillery and machine guns in fortified positions. The Second Army suffered a heavy defeat at Morhange, and the First Army had to withdraw from Sarrebourg. Among those killed in the fighting was a son of General Castelnau. The French fell back across the border to the heights in front of Nancy, where they halted the German pursuit.

## Significance

The offensive in Lorraine was the first great test of the French doctrine of the offensive at all costs, and it ended in its bloody failure. French losses were enormous. The defeat at Morhange and Sarrebourg, and a few days later in the Ardennes, forced Joffre to change his plans. Combined with the German thrust through Belgium, this led to the Great Retreat, which ended only on the Marne.
