On 17 August 1914 the government of [Belgium](/kraje/belgia) left [Brussels](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruksela) and moved to [Antwerp](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpia). After the fall of the forts of Liège the German army was free to advance into the interior of the country, and the capital lay directly in its path.

## The fortress on the Scheldt

Antwerp was Belgium's largest port and the country's strongest fortress. It was surrounded by rings of forts, and pre-war plans envisaged that in the event of invasion it would become the last bastion of defence, the so-called national redoubt. From Antwerp the Belgian army could threaten the flank and rear of the German forces marching on France, and through the port it could keep in touch with Britain.

The Belgian field army, commanded in person by King [Albert I](/postacie/albert-i), was also falling back on the fortress. The King judged it better to keep the army intact than to risk its destruction in open battle against superior German forces.

## Brussels an open city

There was no intention of defending Brussels. It was declared an open city to spare it destruction and casualties among its inhabitants. On 20 August German troops entered the Belgian capital. For many hours long columns of soldiers marched through the city before the eyes of its silent inhabitants, and Brussels came under an occupation that lasted until November 1918.

## Significance

Antwerp held out until October 1914. After its [siege](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblężenie_Antwerpii) the Belgian army withdrew westwards to the river Yser, where it held on to a small strip of unoccupied territory. The government then moved to France, to [Sainte-Adresse](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sainte-Adresse) near Le Havre, where it remained until the end of the war. King Albert I commanded the army at the front for four years, becoming a symbol of Belgian resistance.
