On 31 October 1914 the British Secretary of State for War, [Lord Herbert Kitchener](/postacie/horatio-kitchener), sent a message via [Cairo](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kair) to Emir [Abdullah](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_Allah_I_ibn_Husajn), son of [Hussein bin Ali](/postacie/husajn-ibn-ali), the [Sharif](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szarif) of [Mecca](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mekka). In it he promised the Arabs protection and help if they sided with Britain in the coming war with Turkey. It was the first step on the road that led to the outbreak of the Arab Revolt in 1916.

## Earlier contacts

Hussein, as Sharif and Emir of Mecca, exercised authority over the holy cities of Islam in the [Hejaz](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidżaz) on behalf of the Sultan. His relations with the Young Turk government in Constantinople were strained. The Sharif feared that the government, by building a railway to Mecca and strengthening the Ottoman governors, would strip him of his power. In February and April 1914 his son Abdullah visited Cairo and talked to Kitchener, then British Consul-General in Egypt, and to his Oriental Secretary, [Ronald Storrs](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Storrs). He asked whether Britain would help his father if the Turks tried to remove him. The answer at the time was no: in Arabia the British were interested only in the safety of pilgrims from India.

The war changed the situation. On 24 September 1914 Kitchener, by now Secretary of State for War, instructed Storrs to send a trusted messenger to Abdullah. He was to find out whether, if Turkey were dragged into the war by Germany, the Sharif and the Arabs of the Hejaz would be "with us or against us". The messenger returned to Cairo on 30 October with a cautious but friendly reply. Abdullah wrote that the people of the Hejaz would accept a closer association with Britain if it defended the rights of their country and of the Emir against foreign aggression, especially Ottoman.

## The promise

Kitchener replied at once, on the day the Admiralty [ordered hostilities against Turkey to begin](/brytyjski-rozkaz-dzialan-przeciw-turcji). He wrote that if the Arab nation assisted England in the war that Turkey had forced upon it, England would guarantee that no intervention took place in Arabia and would give the Arabs every assistance against external aggression. He added that perhaps the caliphate might pass to "an Arab of true race" at Mecca or Medina. This was an allusion to the title of [caliph](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalifat), which was borne by the Ottoman Sultan. The British feared that the Sultan-Caliph would call on Muslims to wage holy war.

The message reached Mecca in a version drafted in Cairo, dated in some sources to 1 November. It was more extensive than the London one. It spoke of a guarantee of the independence, rights and privileges of the Sharifate against all foreign aggression, especially Ottoman, and of Britain's undertaking not to interfere in religious matters. Hussein was not yet ready to break with the Sultan and gave an evasive reply, but Kitchener's letter became a point of reference for further talks. In 1915–1916 the Sharif conducted a correspondence with the British High Commissioner in Egypt, Henry McMahon, and in June 1916 the [Arab Revolt](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powstanie_arabskie_(1916–1918)) broke out.
