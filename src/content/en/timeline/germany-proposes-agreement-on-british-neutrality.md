On the evening of 29 July 1914 Chancellor [Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg](/postacie/theobald-von-bethmann-hollweg), having returned from a conference with the Emperor in [Potsdam](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poczdam), summoned the British ambassador, Sir Edward Goschen. He made him a proposal intended to secure for [Germany](/kraje/niemcy) the neutrality of [Great Britain](/kraje/wielka-brytania) in the event of a European war.

The Chancellor gave assurances that if Britain remained neutral, Germany, after a victorious war, would not seek territorial gains at the expense of [France](/kraje/francja) in Europe. When asked about the French [colonies](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francuskie_imperium_kolonialne), however, he would not give a similar promise. He also declared that Germany would respect the neutrality of the [Netherlands](/kraje/holandia), provided other states did so too. As for [Belgium](/kraje/belgia), he said only that its territory would remain intact after the war if it did not side against Germany. Bethmann Hollweg presented these assurances as a possible starting point for a broader neutrality agreement between the two countries.

The proposal revealed more than the Chancellor intended. If Germany was already setting the terms of peace with France and would not guarantee Belgium's integrity during the war, it meant that it was reckoning on an attack on France, including through Belgian territory. For the British, Belgian neutrality, guaranteed by the [Treaty of London of 1839](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traktat_londyński_(1839)), was a matter of principle.

The Chancellor did not know that the same afternoon in London the Foreign Secretary, [Sir Edward Grey](/postacie/edward-grey), had warned the German ambassador, Prince [Karl Max Lichnowsky](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karol_Lichnowsky), that if France were drawn into the war, Britain could not stand aside for long. The report of this conversation reached Berlin only after the meeting with Goschen and seriously alarmed the German leadership.

On 30 July Grey rejected the German proposal. He held that Britain could not bind itself to neutrality on such terms, and that concluding such a bargain at the expense of France and Belgium would be a disgrace for it. The Chancellor's attempt had the opposite effect: instead of reassuring London, it strengthened British suspicions about Germany's real intentions.
