On 23 August 1914 the new commander of the German Eighth Army, General [Paul von Hindenburg](/postacie/paul-von-hindenburg), arrived at its headquarters in [Marienburg](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malbork). With him came the new chief of staff, General [Erich Ludendorff](/postacie/erich-ludendorff). They replaced General Maximilian von Prittwitz, who after the Battle of Gumbinnen had wanted to abandon East Prussia to the Russians.

## The pensioner from Hanover

Hindenburg, born in 1847 in [Posen](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poznań), was a veteran of the wars against Austria and France. In 1911 he had retired and was living in Hanover. He was 66 when, on 22 August, he received a telegram asking whether he was ready to take command. He replied briefly that he was ready. That same night he boarded a special train in which Ludendorff was already waiting for him.

Ludendorff was at that time one of the ablest officers of the German General Staff. A few days earlier he had distinguished himself at Liège, where he had taken command of a brigade and occupied the citadel. He was to be the brain of the new command, and Hindenburg its calm, confidence-inspiring figure of authority.

## A ready-made plan

At Marienburg the new commanders found a plan prepared by an Eighth Army staff officer, Lieutenant Colonel [Max Hoffmann](/postacie/max-hoffmann). It provided for leaving a weak screen against Rennenkampf's army and sending the main forces south, against Samsonov's army. Hindenburg and Ludendorff adopted it almost unchanged. A few days later it brought the Germans a great victory at Tannenberg.

## The beginning of a legend

Tannenberg made Hindenburg a national hero. In 1916, together with Ludendorff, he took over the direction of the entire German army, and in 1925 he was elected President of the Reich. As president, in January 1933 he appointed Adolf Hitler chancellor.
