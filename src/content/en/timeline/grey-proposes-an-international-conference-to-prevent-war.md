On 24 July 1914 the British Foreign Secretary, Sir [Edward Grey](/postacie/edward-grey), made one of the most important attempts to halt the July Crisis by diplomatic means. Faced with the extremely harsh Austro-Hungarian ultimatum to [Serbia](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Królestwo_Serbii), Grey concluded that an ordinary exchange of notes between capitals would not be enough. He therefore proposed convening an international conference that could take the initiative before the conflict spiralled out of control.

The British idea envisaged mediation by four powers not directly involved in the Austro-Serbian dispute: [Great Britain](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zjednoczone_Królestwo_Wielkiej_Brytanii_i_Irlandii), [France](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/III_Republika_Francuska), [Germany](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesarstwo_Niemieckie) and [Italy](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Królestwo_Włoch_(1861–1946)). Grey hoped that such a group could mediate between [Vienna](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiedeń) and [St Petersburg](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersburg) while reducing the risk of immediate military escalation. In practice the aim was to create a brief political buffer that would allow time for negotiation and for tension to ease after the ultimatum of 23 July.

Grey's proposal did not mean that London was taking Serbia's side. Rather, the British minister was trying to preserve the peace of Europe while keeping Britain's traditional role as mediator. He assumed that Britain could influence [Russia](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperium_Rosyjskie) to restrain Belgrade, while Germany should use its own influence to hold back Vienna. As London saw it, only joint pressure on both sides could still prevent a Balkan crisis from turning into a war between the great powers.

The problem was that the initiative came at a moment when Berlin was officially speaking of peace but in reality was backing a policy of localising the conflict on terms favourable to [Austria-Hungary](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Węgry). The German leadership did not want an open European forum that might soften the ultimatum or force Vienna into concessions. That is why Grey's proposal met with no real support where it was most needed.

Even so, 24 July 1914 remains a moment when there was still a genuine attempt to avert catastrophe through diplomacy. Grey understood well that if Austro-Hungarian troops entered Serbia, the conflict would soon cease to be local. His proposal for a conference was an attempt to regain control of events before the fate of Europe was decided by ultimatum deadlines, mobilisations and alliance obligations.
