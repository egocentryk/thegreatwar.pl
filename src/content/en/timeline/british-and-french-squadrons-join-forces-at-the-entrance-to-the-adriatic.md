On 15 August 1914 British and French squadrons joined forces at the entrance to the [Adriatic Sea](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morze_Adriatyckie). Three days after Britain and France declared war on Austria-Hungary, the Allies began joint operations against the Habsburg fleet.

## Joint command

The union of forces put into effect the naval convention concluded in London on 6 August. Under it, command of the Allied fleets in the Mediterranean passed to the French admiral Augustin Boué de Lapeyrère. France contributed the main forces to the combined squadron, including modern battleships, and the British the cruisers and destroyers that had remained in the Mediterranean after the escape of the *Goeben* and *Breslau* to the Dardanelles.

## Blockade of the Adriatic

The Adriatic was the sea on which the whole fleet of [Austria-Hungary](/kraje/austro-wegry) was based, with its main ports at Pola and in the Bay of Kotor. Its only exit to the Mediterranean led through the [Strait of Otranto](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cieśnina_Otranto) between Italy and Albania. By controlling the strait the Allies could shut the Austro-Hungarian fleet up in the Adriatic and secure the troop transports from North Africa to France.

The Allies also wanted to help Montenegro, whose coast was being blockaded and shelled by Austro-Hungarian warships. The very next day, 16 August, the combined fleet sailed deep into the Adriatic and sank the Austro-Hungarian cruiser *Zenta* off the Montenegrin coast.

## Significance

The blockade of the Adriatic became one of the Allies' main tasks in the Mediterranean right up to the end of the war. The Austro-Hungarian fleet rarely left its bases, and engagements in the Adriatic were usually small in scale. After Italy entered the war in 1915, the Allies built a barrage of nets and patrols in the Strait of Otranto, intended also to stop German and Austro-Hungarian submarines.
