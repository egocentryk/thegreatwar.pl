On 4 June 1916 (22 May by the Julian calendar then used in Russia) the Russian South-Western Front began a great offensive against Austria-Hungary. At dawn the Russian batteries opened fire along the whole front, from the [Styr](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Styr) in Volhynia to [Czernowitz](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czerniowce) in Bukovina. The Austrian official history writes that on the most important sectors the bombardment reached an intensity the Eastern Front had never yet known. That day all four armies of General [Aleksei Brusilov](/postacie/aleksiej-brusilow) began their attack: in Volhynia with a bombardment intended to prepare the assault for the following day, near Tarnopol and on the Dniester with infantry attacks as well. On the Austro-Hungarian side it happened to be the 60th birthday of Archduke [Friedrich](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fryderyk_Habsburg), the nominal commander-in-chief of the army.

## Attack on every sector

Russian plans for the summer of 1916 envisaged that the main blow would be struck by the Western Front towards Vilna, while the South-Western Front, which Brusilov had [commanded since April](/brusilow-dowodca-frontu-poludniowo-zachodniego), was to pin down the enemy and make a subsidiary attack towards Lutsk. The date was brought forward by the Austrian [offensive in the Tyrol](/bitwy/bitwa-pod-asiago), after which the Italians begged the Russians for help. The Chief of Staff to the Supreme Commander, General [Mikhail Alekseyev](/postacie/michail-aleksiejew), set Brusilov's attack for 4 June and the Western Front's for 10–11 June. Brusilov did not want to repeat the mistakes of the [March offensive at Lake Naroch](/bitwy/bitwa-nad-jeziorem-narocz), where an attack concentrated on a single sector had allowed the Germans to bring up reserves in time. Each of his armies therefore prepared its own breakthrough, and the enemy, seeing preparations along the whole front, did not know where the main blow would fall.

Alekseyev regarded such a plan as a dispersal of forces. According to Brusilov's memoirs, as late as the evening of 3 June he urged him by telephone, invoking the wishes of [Nicholas II](/postacie/mikolaj-ii), to postpone the attack for a few days and concentrate his forces in one place. Brusilov refused and offered to be relieved. Alekseyev replied that the Tsar was already asleep and it would not be proper to wake him, and ended the conversation with the words: "God be with you. Act as you see fit." In the morning Austrian wireless operators intercepted Brusilov's order of the day to his troops: "The time has come to drive out the vile enemy. All the armies of our front are attacking simultaneously."

## Volhynia: a day of bombardment

The main blow was to be struck by the [8th Army](/sily-zbrojne/8-armia-rosyjska) of General [Aleksei Kaledin](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksiej_Kaledin) east of [Lutsk](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Łuck), where the Austro-Hungarian 4th Army of Archduke [Joseph Ferdinand](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Józef_Ferdynand) stood on the defensive. Kaledin concentrated about a hundred battalions and 320 guns on the sector of attack. From 4 a.m. the Russian artillery methodically destroyed the wire, breastworks and trenches of the 2nd Infantry Division and the 70th Honvéd Division west of [Olyka](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ołyka) and shelled Austrian headquarters. The defenders survived the bombardment in deep dugouts and suffered moderate losses, but their positions were wrecked in many places. At about 5 p.m. Russian units burst out of their trenches in strong reconnaissance attacks, which the Austrians took for the real assault and repulsed with barrage fire, using up ammunition. In the north the Russian XXX Corps attacked the Austro-Hungarian 4th Infantry Division without success from half past four until 8 p.m. and, according to Russian figures, lost about 1,900 men. In the evening the 4th Army command reported with satisfaction that all units had held their positions. Kaledin, who was to decide himself on the moment of the assault, set it for 9 a.m. the next day.

## 11th Army: Cebrów and Sapanów

The [11th Army](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/11_Armia_(Imperium_Rosyjskie)) of General [Vladimir Sakharov](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Władimir_Sacharow) was above all to pin down the enemy. At 11 a.m. its VI Corps, with 13 armoured cars waiting behind it, attacked along the railway from [Tarnopol](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarnopol) to Lviv the positions of the Budapest 32nd Infantry Division near [Cebrów](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cebrów). The Russians broke in places as far as the second line, but in night counter-attacks the Hungarians recovered almost all their trenches. According to Russian figures the VI Corps lost about 5,000 men that day. Greater success attended the attack of two regiments of the Russian 3rd Infantry Division near [Sapanów](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapanów) on the Ikva, on the sector of the Austro-Hungarian 1st Army. The Viennese Landsturm men retook their trenches, but north of them the Russians held on in a bend of the river. According to Russian figures, in the fighting at Sapanów on 4 and 5 June they took almost 3,000 prisoners. Fearing a breakthrough, the Austro-Hungarian command sent its reserve, the 25th Infantry Division, in that direction, taking it away from Lutsk, where it was soon to be needed.

## The Dniester: the first breach

The most dangerous situation arose on 4 June at the southern end of the front. The [9th Army](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/9_Armia_(Imperium_Rosyjskie)) of General Platon Lechitsky attacked the Austro-Hungarian [7th Army](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_Armia_(austro-węgierska)) of General [Karl Pflanzer-Baltin](/postacie/karl-pflanzer-baltin) east of Czernowitz, between the [Dniester](/miejsca/dniestr) and the Prut. For weeks the Russians had been sapping towards the Austrian positions, and in front of some divisions their assault trenches had come within a few dozen paces of the wire. From 4 a.m. their artillery kept switching targets to deceive the defenders, and finally concentrated its fire on the positions of the Hungarian 79th Honvéd Brigade. When the fire suddenly stopped at noon, the Russian infantry reached the trenches within moments, before the defenders had time to leave their dugouts. The brigade was shattered, and only about 600 of its men came back. The Russians claimed to have taken 7,000 prisoners here.

In the afternoon the Russian XI Corps broke into the village of Dobronouc (Dobronăuți), and the 3rd Zaamur Division broke through the positions of the 42nd Honvéd Division on the Dniester. Two Croatian regiments of that division were almost destroyed, and the Russians took more than 4,000 prisoners there. The Russians penetrated 5 kilometres deep into the Austrian positions, and at about 5 p.m. the village of Okna fell. The Austrians threw all their reserves into a counter-attack and by nightfall had retaken Dobronouc and Okna, but did not recover the lost trenches. General Benigni's group lost a third of its guns that day, and its soldiers tried to dig in in the open. Pflanzer-Baltin, confined to bed with fever at his headquarters in Kolomea, did not yet realise the extent of the defeat. On the sector of the [7th Army](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_Armia_(Imperium_Rosyjskie)) of General [Dmitry Shcherbachev](/postacie/dmitrij-szczerbaczow) on the lower Strypa, 4 June saw only bombardment; the assault was planned for 6 June.

## What the following days brought

On the evening of 4 June the Austro-Hungarian command in Volhynia could still believe that the front had held. The army group commander, General [Alexander von Linsingen](/postacie/alexander-von-linsingen), wrote that day of "weak attacks" with which the Russians sought to offset the Austrian successes in the Tyrol. The next day Kaledin's infantry went over to the assault near Olyka and within a few hours broke through the first and second lines of defence of the 4th Army. On the evening of 7 June the Russians entered Lutsk. So began the offensive that brought the Russian army its greatest victory of the war.
