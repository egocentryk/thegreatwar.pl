With the arrival in Mombasa of Indian Expeditionary Force "B", its commander, Major-General Arthur Aitken, became commander-in-chief of all British forces in East Africa. The British chronology of the war records this change under 31 October 1914, when a [conference](/indyjski-korpus-ekspedycyjny-b-w-mombasie) of his staff with the local commanders was held in Mombasa.

Until then the British forces in [British East Africa](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brytyjska_Afryka_Wschodnia) had been commanded [since September](/general-stewart-dowodca-w-afryce-wschodniej) by Brigadier-General James Marshall Stewart, commander of Indian Expeditionary Force "C". His troops, about 4,000 men, were primarily to defend the railway from Mombasa into the interior. Aitken had come with a different mission: with Force "B" he was to conquer the whole of [German East Africa](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niemiecka_Afryka_Wschodnia). Both operations therefore had to be brought under one command, and Aitken was the senior in rank.

Stewart did not lose his force, but from then on he was subordinate to Aitken. In the plan agreed in Mombasa he was given a supporting role. At the same time as the landing at Tanga, his troops were to attack the German positions at Longido, north-west of [Kilimanjaro](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilimandżaro), and draw part of the enemy's forces there.

Aitken did not hold supreme command for long. At the beginning of November both attacks, at Tanga and at Longido, ended in failure. After the defeat at Tanga Aitken was recalled, and command in East Africa passed to General Richard Wapshare. The two Indian forces were merged and for the following months defended British East Africa. Aitken returned to England, and in 1915 he was reduced to the rank of colonel. Only after the war was he cleared, and in 1921 he was restored to the honorary rank of brigadier-general.
