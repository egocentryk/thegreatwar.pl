On 7 September 1914 the governor of the French fortress of [Maubeuge](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maubeuge), General Joseph Fournier, asked the Germans for a truce and agreed terms of capitulation. The fortress on the Sambre, right by the Belgian border, had been holding out since the end of August. The capitulation came into effect on 8 September.

## A fortress encircled

Maubeuge was left on its own when, after the defeats at Charleroi and Mons, the French and British forces withdrew to the south. [At the end of August the German Second Army surrounded the fortress](/poczatek-oblezenia-maubeuge), and its capture was entrusted to General Hans von Zwehl and his VII Reserve Corps. Heavy siege artillery was brought from Namur, including German 420 mm and Austro-Hungarian 305 mm mortars.

The bombardment began on 29 August. The forts, built mostly in the 1880s, could not withstand hits from the heaviest shells. From 3–4 September the shelling intensified, and German infantry forced their way through breaches in the ring of defences. The garrison, made up largely of older reservists, attempted sorties but failed to break the encirclement.

## The end of resistance

By the beginning of September most of the forts and fortifications on the north-eastern side of the fortress had been destroyed or taken by the Germans. On the evening of 6 September a council of war called by Fournier concluded that further resistance was impossible. On 7 September the governor opened negotiations, and terms of capitulation were agreed the same day. The fortress was handed over to the Germans on 8 September.

Almost the entire garrison went into captivity. Sources differ on the number of prisoners, giving figures from about 32,000–33,000 to over 40,000 men. The Germans also captured several hundred guns, some of them damaged. Fournier spent the rest of the war as a prisoner. In 1920, like every commandant who had surrendered a fortress, he was tried by court martial. He was accused, among other things, of not destroying the guns and ammunition before the capitulation. The court acquitted him.

## Significance

Maubeuge fell in the days when the fate of the campaign was being decided on the Marne. Its resistance was not in vain, however. For almost two weeks the fortress tied down considerable German forces and blocked an important railway line from Belgium into France, hampering the supply of the German right-wing armies. The VII Reserve Corps, released after the capitulation, did not arrive in time for the Battle of the Marne. It reached the Aisne on 13 September, just in time to close the gap in the front of the retreating Germans.
