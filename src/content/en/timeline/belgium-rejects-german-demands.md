On 3 August 1914 at 7 a.m., exactly when the deadline expired, the government of [Belgium](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgia) delivered to Germany its reply to the ultimatum of the previous evening. It was a firm refusal. Belgium did not agree to the passage of German troops through its territory and announced that it would defend itself.

The decision was taken during the night at a meeting of the government and political leaders called by King [Albert I](/postacie/albert-i). Despite the awareness that the Belgian army was incomparably weaker than the German one, no one spoke in favour of giving way. The reply stated that accepting the German proposals would mean sacrificing the honour of the nation and betraying its duties towards Europe. Belgium recalled that its neutrality was guaranteed by the powers, Prussia among them, and announced that it would repel any attack on its rights by every means at its disposal.

On the same day King Albert appealed to King [George V](/postacie/jerzy-v) of Great Britain for diplomatic support in defence of Belgian neutrality. The government in Brussels did not yet call for military help from the guarantors. It wanted there to be no doubt that it was Germany that would be the first to violate the Belgian border.

The refusal had enormous moral and political significance. A small state had rejected the demands of the most powerful army in Europe, knowing that it faced invasion and destruction. In Britain and in other countries Belgium's stance aroused widespread admiration and became one of the main arguments for London's entry into the war.

The next day, on the morning of 4 August, German troops crossed the Belgian border. The Belgian army, mobilised a few days earlier, went into action in defence of the country, and the first heavy fighting flared up around the forts of [Liège](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liège).
