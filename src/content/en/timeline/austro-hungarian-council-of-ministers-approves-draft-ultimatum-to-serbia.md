On 19 July 1914 the Austro-Hungarian council of ministers approved the draft ultimatum that was to be handed to [Serbia](/kraje/serbia) a few days later. This was the moment when earlier political decisions took on a more final, concrete diplomatic form. The point was not merely to present [Belgrade](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgrad) with harsh demands, but to choose their content so that Serbia would find itself in an almost hopeless position.

[Vienna](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiedeń) wanted the document to appear a formally justified response to the [Sarajevo assassination](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zamach_w_Sarajewie), while at the same time containing conditions hard to reconcile with the sovereignty of the Serbian state. In practice this meant that the ultimatum was to give [Austria-Hungary](/kraje/austro-wegry) a political and moral justification for subsequent military action. From the point of view of the monarchy's decision-makers, the ideal outcome was not a peaceful settlement but Belgrade's rejection or only partial acceptance of the demands.

The approval of the draft on 19 July also showed how closely Vienna's actions were tied to Berlin's calculations. [Germany](/kraje/niemcy) continued to support a hard line towards Serbia, but it wanted the whole operation to be played out so that responsibility for escalation would fall above all on Austria-Hungary and Serbia rather than on Berlin itself. That is why not only the content of the ultimatum mattered, but also the moment of its delivery and the way it would be presented to the European capitals.

It was no accident that delivery of the note was postponed until 23 July. Vienna and Berlin reckoned that the right timing would hinder a swift joint response by [France](/kraje/francja) and [Russia](/kraje/rosja), while allowing the conflict to be presented as a local Austro-Serbian dispute. Political calculation of this kind was one of the most characteristic features of the July Crisis: the great powers not only took decisions but also carefully stage-managed their sequence and presentation.

That is why 19 July 1914 should be regarded as one of the last stages before an open European crisis. After that date the question was no longer whether Serbia would receive an ultimatum, but when exactly it would be delivered and how other states would react to a document drafted so that it would be hard to accept in full.
