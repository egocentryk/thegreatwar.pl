On 18 August 1914, on the river Gete in central [Belgium](/kraje/belgia), the Belgian army clashed with the main body of the German First Army. The fighting, which lasted until 19 August, ended a three-week attempt to stop the Germans in the open field. In the evening King [Albert I](/postacie/albert-i), who commanded the army in person, ordered a retreat to the fortress of Antwerp.

## Waiting on the Gete

After the Germans entered Belgium, the field army had assembled by 6 August in the quadrilateral between Tienen, Leuven, Wavre and Perwez, two days' march west of Liège. The Belgian staff decided to wait there for the French and the British. The line of the Gete, with its left flank resting on the river Demer, covered a large part of the country and Brussels, and at the same time did not cut the army off from Antwerp, where all its supplies had been collected. In the first line, from the area of [Tienen](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tienen) (French: Tirlemont) to [Jodoigne](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jodoigne), stood the 1st and 5th Divisions, with the 3rd Division, back from Liège, between them. The 2nd Division was in the second line near [Leuven](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leuven), the 6th Division further south, and the left flank as far as [Diest](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diest) was covered by the cavalry division. The 4th Division was defending the fortress of Namur.

From 10 August German cavalry backed by Jäger roamed in front of the Belgian lines. On 12 August six German cavalry regiments with infantry and artillery tried to force the Gete at Halen. The Belgian cavalry division, supported in the afternoon by an infantry brigade, drove them back after a day-long fight in the [Battle of Halen](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitwa_pod_Haelen). The Belgian official account noted, however, that it was only an episode. When [the last forts of Liège fell](/padaja-ostatnie-forty-liege) on 16 August, further German corps were crossing the Meuse, including over the repaired bridge at Huy. The German Supreme Command ordered [Alexander von Kluck](/postacie/alexander-von-kluck)'s First Army and [Karl von Bülow](/postacie/karl-von-bulow)'s Second Army to deal with the Belgian army and force it away from Antwerp. On 17 August, as the Germans drew near, [the government left Brussels](/rzad-belgii-przenosi-sie-do-antwerpii).

## 18 August

In the morning the Germans attacked along the whole Belgian front. On the left flank, at seven o'clock, they attacked Budingen and Geetbets, held by two squadrons of the Guides, and at ten their infantry crossed the river. Halen was shelled by artillery, and at Diest two platoons of cyclists and a company of engineers held off an entire brigade for an hour and a half. The cavalry division had to fall back to the north, and the 2nd Division was sent to take its place.

Further south a whole German corps attacked the 1st Division. The Germans took Tienen and struck the positions at Sint-Margriete-Houtem (French: Hautem-Sainte-Marguerite) in front and flank. According to the Belgian official account, the 2nd Brigade held out stubbornly until late in the evening and suffered heavy losses, but enabled the rest of the division to disengage.

The Belgian staff no longer had any illusions. According to the British official history, the German II, IV and IX Corps stood opposite the Belgian left between Diest and Tienen, while the Guard Corps and the VII and X Corps were marching against the right, from Jodoigne to Namur, followed by reserve corps. The Belgians estimated that some half a million men were approaching north of the Meuse. The Belgian army was the size of two army corps, and Allied help was far away. The French Fifth Army was only just coming up to the Sambre, and of the British only the cavalry division had detrained near Maubeuge. Their army was to be ready to march on 22 August at the earliest.

## The King's decision

In the afternoon of 18 August the King decided to withdraw the army to the north-west. At 7.30 p.m. the orders were issued: at dawn the divisions were to cross to the left bank of the river Dyle and take up the line from Neerijse through Leuven to Rotselaar. To stay on the Gete would have meant a battle whose outcome, as the Belgian account put it, was not in doubt. The army would have been attacked in front and on both flanks, and whatever remained of it would have been cut off from Antwerp.

The decision meant giving up Brussels without a fight. The King judged, however, that preserving the army, the state's only armed force, mattered more than holding the capital. The Belgians also pointed out that for thirteen days, from 5 to 18 August, they had forced the Germans into a wide outflanking movement that had cost the enemy valuable time.

## What came next

At dawn on 19 August the rearguard of the 3rd Division fought a hard action at Aarschot against the German II Corps. It became clear that the Germans were outflanking the Belgian left, the line of the Dyle became untenable, and the army made straight for [Antwerp](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpia). It reached the fortress on 20 August without being seriously troubled by the enemy. That same day the Germans entered Brussels. From the fortress the Belgian army threatened the flank and rear of the Germans marching on France, tying down considerable forces that they later lacked on the Marne. Antwerp held out until October, and after its fall the Belgian army fought on along the Yser.
