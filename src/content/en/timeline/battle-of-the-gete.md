On 18 August 1914, on the river Gete in central [Belgium](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgia), the Belgian army clashed with the advancing German forces. The fighting, which lasted until 19 August, was part of the struggle for Antwerp and decided the further strategy of King [Albert I](/postacie/albert-i).

## The line on the Gete

After the fall of Liège the Belgian field army took up defensive positions on the river Gete, east of Brussels, on a line from [Tienen](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tienen) (French: Tirlemont) to [Jodoigne](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jodoigne). It was to cover the capital and wait for the arrival of French and British troops. A few days earlier, on 12 August, the Belgians had repulsed German cavalry at the [Battle of Halen](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitwa_pod_Haelen), which raised their morale.

This time, however, they faced the main forces of the German 1st Army, marching through Belgium into France. On 18 August the Germans attacked the Belgian positions, especially around Tienen and the nearby villages. The Belgian divisions defended themselves stubbornly, suffering heavy losses.

## The King's decision

King Albert I, who commanded the army in person, quickly realised that the German superiority was overwhelming and that Allied help would not arrive in time. Staying on the Gete threatened the encirclement and destruction of the whole army. On the evening of 18 August the King decided to retreat to Antwerp, the strongest fortress in the country, designated as the last bastion of defence.

It was a difficult decision, because it meant giving up Brussels without a fight. The King judged, however, that preserving the army mattered more than holding the capital. From Antwerp the Belgian army could threaten the rear of the Germans marching on France and maintain communications with Britain.

## Significance

The fighting on the Gete ended the period in which the Belgians tried to stop the Germans in the open field. The army withdrew to [Antwerp](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerpia), and on 20 August the Germans entered Brussels. Albert I's decision, however, saved the Belgian army, which for the following weeks tied down substantial German forces from Antwerp and, after the fortress fell in October, fought on along the Yser.
