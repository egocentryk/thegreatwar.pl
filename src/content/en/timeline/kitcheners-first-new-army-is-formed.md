On 21 August 1914 the British War Office issued Army Order No. 324, ordering the formation of the first of the so-called New Armies. It was to consist of six infantry divisions, formed from the volunteers who had come forward to enlist after the outbreak of war.

## Kitchener's vision

Before the war the [British Army](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Army) was a small professional army, scattered across the whole empire. The new Secretary of State for War, [Lord Kitchener](/postacie/horatio-kitchener), unlike most politicians, did not believe in a short war. He thought it would last for years and that Britain would have to raise an army numbering millions of soldiers. On 7 August he issued an appeal for the first hundred thousand volunteers aged between 19 and 30.

The response exceeded expectations. Long queues formed outside the recruiting offices, and at the end of August and the beginning of September as many as several tens of thousands of men a day were enlisting.

## The first hundred thousand

The order of 21 August gave the volunteers an organisation. The first New Army, known as "K1" or "the first hundred thousand", comprised the 9th to 14th Divisions, among them a Scottish, an Irish and a Light Division. The new battalions were raised within existing regiments, but training began almost from scratch. There was a shortage of uniforms, weapons, barracks and experienced instructors. Recruits often drilled in civilian clothes and used wooden dummies instead of rifles.

Further New Armies soon followed, among them the famous Pals Battalions, made up of men from the same town, workplace or sports club.

## Significance

The K1 divisions went into action in 1915: some in France, some on the Gallipoli peninsula. The volunteer New Armies became the backbone of the British land forces in 1915–1916 and suffered enormous losses, especially in the Battle of the Somme. When the volunteers' enthusiasm began to wane, Britain in 1916, for the first time in its history, introduced general conscription.
