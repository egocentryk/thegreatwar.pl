On 14 August 1914 the commander-in-chief of the Russian army, [Grand Duke Nicholas Nikolaevich](/postacie/mikolaj-mikolajewicz), issued a proclamation to the Poles. It was the first official announcement since the partitions by one of the partitioning powers that the Polish lands would be united after the war.

## The text of the proclamation

The proclamation began by recalling that a century and a half earlier the living body of Poland had been torn to pieces, but that her soul had survived and lived in the hope of rebirth and of reconciliation with Russia. The Grand Duke announced that after victory Poland would be united under the sceptre of the Tsar and would be reborn, free in her faith, her language and her self-government. He called on the Poles to take up the fight at Russia's side against the common enemy, Germany.

## The struggle for the Poles

The proclamation was a response to a peculiar situation. The Poles lived in three partitions and served in three armies, which were now fighting one another. Each side tried to win their support. In Galicia, under Austrian rule, the Polish rifle units of [Józef Piłsudski](/postacie/jozef-pilsudski) were already active; on 6 August they had marched into the [Kingdom of Poland](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Królestwo_Polskie_(kongresowe)), and on 16 August the [Supreme National Committee](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naczelny_Komitet_Narodowy) was founded in Kraków, supporting the fight alongside Austria-Hungary.

Russia, which for more than a hundred years had suppressed Polish national aspirations, had to offer something in return. The proclamation was meant to counter Austrian and German propaganda and to keep the rear of the front, which ran across Polish lands, quiet.

## Reactions

The proclamation was greeted with enthusiasm by Polish politicians associated with [Roman Dmowski](/postacie/roman-dmowski), who regarded Germany as Poland's main enemy and counted on cooperation with Russia. They soon formed the [Polish National Committee](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Komitet_Narodowy_Polski_(1914–1917)), which supported the Russian cause. Others regarded it with distrust. The proclamation had been issued by the commander of the army, not by the Tsar or the government, and its promises were vague and did not provide for independence.

## Significance

The Russian promises were never fulfilled. In 1915 the Russian army withdrew from the Kingdom of Poland, and these lands were occupied by Germany and Austria-Hungary. The Grand Duke's proclamation was nevertheless an important moment: for the first time since the partitions the Polish question became the object of international rivalry, and the powers began to outbid one another with promises to the Poles. This rivalry opened the way to the recovery of independence in 1918.
