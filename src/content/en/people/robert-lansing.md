Robert Lansing directed American diplomacy for almost the whole war, from June 1915 to February 1920, but he was never its real author. Foreign policy was conducted by President [Woodrow Wilson](/postacie/woodrow-wilson) himself and, alongside him, by his trusted adviser, Colonel [Edward House](/postacie/edward-house). Lansing was a lawyer, a specialist in international law, and the author and signatory of dozens of notes in which the United States argued with Germany over submarine warfare and with Britain over the blockade, and in which in October 1918 it passed on the armistice terms to Germany. Privately he was convinced from 1915 that America ought to side with the Entente, and he did a great deal to bring that about. In Paris in 1919 he did not believe in the League of Nations or in national self-determination, which in the end cost him his post.

## Before the war

He was born on 17 October 1864 in Watertown, New York, into a family of lawyers with long roots in the state. He graduated from Amherst College in 1886 and from 1889 worked in the family law firm in Watertown. In 1890 he married Eleanor Foster, daughter of [John W. Foster](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Foster), Secretary of State under Benjamin Harrison, who introduced his son-in-law to the world of diplomacy. His nephews by marriage were the Dulles brothers, of whom [John Foster Dulles](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Foster_Dulles) became Secretary of State years later and Allen head of intelligence.

Lansing specialised in international disputes settled by arbitration. He represented the United States in the Bering Sea fur-seal arbitration in 1892–1893, before the tribunal that fixed the Alaska boundary in 1903, and at The Hague in the North Atlantic fisheries dispute in 1909–1910. He co-founded the American Society of International Law and its journal. He was a conservative Democrat, a pedantic, reserved and taciturn man who in his spare time drew caricatures and wrote poems. In April 1914 he became Counselor of the State Department, the second man in the department, then headed by the pacifist [William Jennings Bryan](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Bryan).

## Counselor: the neutrality disputes

Bryan knew nothing of international law and was often away, so the drafting of notes on neutrality fell to Lansing. In the autumn of 1914, as acting Secretary of State, he conducted the dispute with London over the rules of naval warfare. On 22 October he [withdrew the American proposal](/nota-usa-w-sprawie-prawa-wojny-morskiej) that the belligerents apply the Declaration of London of 1909 and announced that the United States would defend its rights case by case. Soon, however, he began to see the war differently from his superior. When an American was killed in the spring of 1915 on the [sunken *Falaba*](/zatopienie-falaby), he argued for a firm protest to Germany. After the [sinking of the *Lusitania*](/zatopienie-lusitanii) on 7 May 1915 he came, as he later wrote, to the conviction that America would ultimately become Britain's ally.

## Secretary of State

In June 1915 Bryan refused to sign the second, sharp *Lusitania* note and resigned. Lansing sent it on 9 June as acting secretary, and on 23 June Wilson appointed him [Secretary of State](/robert-lansing-sekretarzem-stanu). The choice was not accidental. Lansing had no political following of his own, and House advised the President to pick someone who did not have "too many ideas of his own". Wilson intended in practice to be his own foreign minister and often treated Lansing as a clerk for drafting notes. He typed important texts himself and handed them to the Secretary of State to send.

Within these limits, however, Lansing had considerable influence. He made sure that the dispute with London over the blockade did not go beyond an exchange of notes, while every German offence became a public matter. After the [sinking of the *Arabic*](/zatopienie-statku-arabic) in August 1915 it was to him that the German ambassador [Johann Heinrich von Bernstorff](/postacie/johann-heinrich-von-bernstorff) gave on 1 September a written assurance that submarines [would not sink liners without warning](/niemcy-ustepuja-w-sprawie-wojny-podwodnej). In September, when secret operations in American factories came to light, Lansing demanded the [recall of the Austro-Hungarian ambassador Konstantin Dumba](/usa-zadaja-odwolania-ambasadora-dumby), and in December that of the [German attachés](/usa-zadaja-odwolania-boy-eda-i-papena) Karl Boy-Ed and Franz von Papen. After the [sinking of the Italian liner *Ancona*](/zatopienie-statku-ancona) by a submarine flying the Austro-Hungarian flag he sent a sharp note to Vienna. In September 1915 he argued to the President that without credits for the Entente American exports would collapse, and Wilson stopped opposing large bank loans to London and Paris.

Not all of Lansing's ideas succeeded. In January 1916 he proposed a compromise to the Allies under which merchant ships would give up their armament in exchange for submarines observing the rules of cruiser warfare. The British regarded it as a concession to Germany, and Berlin [announced that it would treat armed merchant ships as warships](/niemiecka-nota-o-uzbrojonych-statkach-handlowych), that is, sink them without warning. On 15 February Lansing had to declare that the United States would not change the existing rules of maritime law during the war, which meant abandoning his idea, and a [fierce dispute](/spor-w-kongresie-o-uzbrojone-statki-handlowe) broke out in Congress over whether to warn Americans against travelling on armed ships. After the [torpedoing of the ferry *Sussex*](/storpedowanie-statku-sussex) Lansing argued for an immediate break in relations with Germany, but Wilson chose an ultimatum, which the Secretary of State [sent on 18 April 1916](/nota-usa-w-sprawie-sussexa). On 4 August 1916 he signed with the Danish minister a treaty for the purchase of the Danish West Indies, which the United States, fearing their seizure by Germany, took over in March 1917 as the [Virgin Islands](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyspy_Dziewicze_Stanów_Zjednoczonych).

## The break and the war

In December 1916 it was Lansing who circulated [Wilson's peace note](/nota-pokojowa-wilsona). He did not believe in it and soon nearly buried it. On 21 December he told journalists that the United States was "drawing nearer the verge of war", which Europe read as a sign that neutrality was being abandoned, and on the same day he had to explain away his words. When Germany [announced unrestricted submarine warfare](/niemcy-oglaszaja-nieograniczona-wojne-podwodna), he urged the President from the first moment to break relations. According to his notes, at the cabinet meeting of 2 February 1917 he argued that a lasting peace required the democratisation of all nations and that an Allied victory was in America's interest. The next day [the United States broke off relations with Germany](/usa-zrywaja-stosunki-z-niemcami), and on 6 April it entered the war.

As Secretary of State of a belligerent country, Lansing handled matters that Wilson left to him more willingly. In March 1917 he [recognised the Russian Provisional Government](/uznanie-rzadu-tymczasowego), the first foreign government to do so. In September he made public intercepted telegrams of German diplomats, including the [Bernstorff telegram](/afera-bernstorffa) asking for money to influence Congress. On 2 November 1917 he concluded with the Japanese envoy Kikujirō Ishii an [agreement on China](/porozumienie-lansing-ishii), in which Washington recognised Japan's "special interests" in China and Tokyo reaffirmed the Open Door principle. Each side understood it in its own way. On 10 November 1917 Lansing instructed the ambassador in Paris to [recognise the Polish National Committee](/stany-zjednoczone-uznaja-komitet-narodowy-polski) as an official Polish organisation. In June 1918 he announced that all the Slav peoples should be completely freed from German and Austrian rule, and this [Lansing declaration](/deklaracja-lansinga) in practice ended American support for preserving the Habsburg monarchy. In September he [recognised the Czechoslovak National Council](/usa-uznaja-czechoslowakow) as a de facto government. Towards the Bolsheviks he was implacable. He regarded them as a threat to the social order of the whole world and opposed their recognition.

In the autumn of 1918 the whole exchange of notes with Germany passed through Lansing's hands. It was he who handed the Swiss chargé d'affaires the President's [first](/pierwsza-odpowiedz-wilsona), [second](/druga-nota-wilsona) and [third](/trzecia-nota-wilsona) replies, and on 5 November the [last note](/nota-lansinga), which conveyed the Allies' reservations on the Fourteen Points and directed the German plenipotentiaries to Marshal [Ferdinand Foch](/postacie/ferdinand-foch).

## Paris

Lansing went to the peace conference as one of the five American plenipotentiaries, but it quickly became clear that Wilson did not take his views into account. The Secretary of State did not believe that the League of Nations in the form proposed by the President could work, and he proposed a more modest organisation based on courts of arbitration. He was sceptical of the principle of self-determination. In private notes of December 1918 he wrote that the phrase was "loaded with dynamite" and would raise hopes that could never be realised. He chaired the commission on responsibility for the war and, together with the other American delegate, opposed bringing Kaiser Wilhelm II before an international tribunal, because in his view there was no law that allowed it. He also judged the finished Treaty of Versailles critically, considering it too harsh and contrary to the promises made, but he did not leave the delegation.

In September 1919 his scepticism came to light. A former member of the delegation, William Bullitt, testified before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee that Lansing had told him the League was "entirely useless", and that if the Senate and the people understood the treaty they would reject it. Wilson, who was then fighting for ratification, regarded this as disloyalty.

## Dismissal

On 2 October 1919 the President suffered a severe stroke. Lansing then summoned the President's doctor and his secretary to explain whether Wilson could perform his duties, and reportedly wondered whether Vice President Thomas Marshall should take them over. Over the following months he called cabinet meetings so that the government could work without the President. In February 1920 Wilson, increasingly suspicious, asked him in a letter whether it was true that he had been calling the cabinet without his knowledge. Lansing replied that he had done so in good faith, and on 12 February he resigned; the resignation was accepted the next day. Public opinion rather took his side, and the manner of his dismissal damaged the President. Lansing's successor was Bainbridge Colby.

## After the war

Lansing returned to legal practice in Washington. In 1921 he published two books on the peace conference, "The Peace Negotiations" and "The Big Four and Others of the Peace Conference", in which he explained his differences with Wilson. He died in Washington on 30 October 1928 and was buried in his family's home town of Watertown. His war memoirs were published posthumously in 1935.

## Assessment

Historians differ in their assessment of Lansing's role. To some he was merely an efficient official in the shadow of the President and House; to others he was one of the men who pushed the United States towards war. The latter point out that as Counselor and Secretary of State he consistently softened disputes with London and sharpened disputes with Berlin, and that as early as the summer of 1915 he regarded a German victory as a threat to America. The German historian Gerhard Ritter called him the coolest head among Wilson's advisers, the least constrained by moral considerations. Wilson's biographer John Milton Cooper, on the other hand, judged that appointing Lansing and treating him as a clerk was one of the President's worst mistakes, because the offended Secretary of State in time began to act behind his back. His scepticism about the League of Nations and self-determination, criticised by Wilson's supporters, has been seen by some later scholars as a sober warning against hopes that the Versailles order failed to fulfil.
