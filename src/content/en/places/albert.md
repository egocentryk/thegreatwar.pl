Albert is a small town on the river Ancre, halfway between [Amiens](/miejsca/amiens) and [Bapaume](/miejsca/bapaume). From the autumn of 1914 it lay just behind the front on the Allied side, and in 1916 it was the main base of the British offensive on the [Somme](/miejsca/somma). Three battles took their name from the town: those of 1914, 1916 and 1918. Soldiers remembered it above all for the "Leaning Virgin" on the basilica tower, and the war left nothing of it but ruins.

## The front outside the town (1914–1915)

In September 1914, during the [race to the sea](/bitwy/wyscig-do-morza), the French and Germans tried to outflank each other north of the Somme. In the [First Battle of Albert](/bitwy/pierwsza-bitwa-pod-albert) the Germans took Bapaume and on 27 September reached Thiepval. The next day they tried to break through to Albert and on towards Amiens, but French infantry and artillery stopped them on the line Maricourt – Fricourt – Thiepval. The front set a few kilometres from the town, and Albert itself, shelled by German artillery, was largely destroyed.

Above the ruins rose the tower of the neo-Byzantine basilica of Notre-Dame de Brebières with its gilded statue of the Virgin and Child. On 15 January 1915 a shell hit the dome, and the statue tilted and hung almost horizontally over the street. Millions of soldiers and picture postcards knew it, and in the trenches it was said that when it fell, the war would end. In the summer of 1915 the British took over the sector at Albert from the French.

## Base of the Battle of the Somme (1916)

The old road to Bapaume ran through Albert, and the 1916 offensive was to go along it. From August 1915 British units and field ambulances buried their dead beside the town cemetery. On 1 July 1916, two minutes before zero hour, the [Lochnagar mine exploded](/wybuch-miny-lochnagar) under the village of La Boisselle on that road, and the 34th (Tyneside) Division advanced east along the highway. North of the road the attack failed almost everywhere. The British called the first two weeks of the offensive the [Battle of Albert](/bitwy/bitwa-pod-albert-1916).

As the front moved east, the town became a great rear hub. From September 1916 field ambulances were concentrated there, and from November the 5th Casualty Clearing Station buried its dead there for two months. Soldiers from Britain, Canada, Australia and India lie in the cemetery.

## The Germans in Albert (1918)

In March 1918 the war came back. During [Operation Michael](/bitwy/operacja-michael) the British 3rd Army's V Corps fell back behind the Ancre on the night of 25–26 March and gave up the ruins of Albert without a fight. On 26 March at about 5 p.m. the Germans entered the town, but fire from British machine guns on the high bank kept them from leaving it westwards. The hungry soldiers found plentiful British supplies in Albert. On 5 April, in Michael's last attack, the Germans tried once more to break through [on the Ancre](/bitwy/bitwa-nad-ancre-1918), but at Albert they were driven back.

According to most sources the statue on the tower fell on 16 April 1918, when British artillery shelled the basilica so that the Germans could not use the tower for observation or as a machine-gun post. The war, however, went on for another seven months, and the statue itself was never found.

On 22 August 1918, the second day of the [Battle of Albert](/bitwy/bitwa-pod-albert-1918), the British 18th Division crossed the Ancre on footbridges thrown over during the night and [retook the town](/brytyjczycy-odbijaja-albert). The 8th East Surreys cleared the ruins, and by 10 a.m. there were hardly any Germans left in Albert. The next day the 38th (Welsh) Division took Usna Hill east of the town. Recaptured Albert had been completely destroyed by artillery fire.

## After the war

The town was rebuilt from scratch, its main streets widened in the process. The basilica was rebuilt to the old plans in 1927–1931, and a faithful copy of the gilded statue was placed on the tower. British war cemeteries grew up around Albert, and the town itself became the starting point for visitors to the Somme battlefield.
